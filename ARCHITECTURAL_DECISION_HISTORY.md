# Architectural Decision History

The "why" behind every major structural decision in this platform, in the order the decisions actually happened, not a reconstructed narrative. Each section answers the specific question this milestone's Workstream 2 asks. Read this before touching any of the systems it describes; the reasoning here is often the only place a non-obvious constraint is written down at all.

## Why Runtime Authority exists

The core problem: an AI agent that can take a real, consequential action (approve a payment, place an order, grant access) has no equivalent, before the fact, of the implicit check a human employee performs before acting on their own authority. Existing categories don't close this gap. AI safety/guardrails work constrains what a model says or generates; it doesn't verify that a specific, real-world action a specific autonomous actor is about to take is one the organization has actually authorized. Enterprise identity systems (Okta, Azure AD, and similar) authenticate a human session; they were never built for a per-transaction, deterministic authority decision about a non-human, autonomous actor. Runtime Authority is the layer built specifically to sit in that gap: after identity is established, before the action executes.

## Why OPA (Open Policy Agent), specifically, and not a custom evaluation engine or an LLM

Two decisions bundled into one, worth separating:

**Why not evaluate policy with a language model**: because the entire value of the product is a decision an auditor can trust was made the same way every time. A model's judgment, however good, is not reproducible in the way a signed record needs to be; "the model decided this was fine" is not something an independent party can verify after the fact, and it is not something the platform can prove was applied consistently across every organization and every request. This is the single most important, non-negotiable architectural boundary in the entire system: **AI never makes an authorization decision at evaluation time.** It may propose candidate policies (Authority Intelligence) for a human to review, but it never decides ALLOW/DENY/HUMAN_REVIEW for a real Intent.

**Why OPA and not a custom-built evaluation engine**: because policy evaluation is exactly the kind of infrastructure a small team should not reimplement. OPA is a mature, independently audited, widely deployed open-source engine, already trusted for policy-as-code at real scale in cloud infrastructure and Kubernetes. Building a custom Rego-equivalent would mean the platform's most safety-critical component was also its least battle-tested one. Every Runtime Policy compiles to real Rego and is evaluated by a real, embedded OPA instance; nothing about this is simulated or approximated.

## Why Runtime Policies exist as their own first-class object, not inline rules

A policy needed a real lifecycle (draft, submitted for review, approved, activated, deprecated, archived, rolled back) because authorization rules in a real enterprise are governed artifacts, not code deployed by whoever last touched the repository. Making a policy change requires the same kind of review and approval real business authority delegation already requires outside software, and the platform's own lifecycle mirrors that discipline rather than inventing a lighter one. The versioning is real (every activation produces a new version, a real compile, a real OPA deployment with a recorded bundle hash) specifically so that "what rule was actually active when this decision was made" is always answerable, not reconstructed from logs.

## Why Canonical Authority (the Authority Graph) exists, separately from Runtime Policies

A Runtime Policy states a rule; the Authority Graph states the underlying *structure of authority* the rule is supposed to reflect: who reports to whom, what resources and operations exist, what delegation, escalation, or inheritance relationships actually hold between named principals. Keeping this separate from the policy layer matters because an organization's authority structure changes independently of any specific rule, and because the structure itself, not just individual rules, is what Authority Intelligence extracts from real documents and what a human reviewer needs to see and correct before any rule derived from it is trusted. Collapsing the two into one concept would make it impossible to ask "does this rule still match our actual delegation structure" as a separate, answerable question.

## Why Authority Intelligence exists, and why it is AI-assisted but never AI-authorized

Manually transcribing an organization's real governance documents (delegation memos, approval matrices, signing schedules) into structured Authority Graph data is slow and doesn't scale past a handful of documents. Authority Intelligence uses a real AI provider (Azure AI Foundry, as of Milestone 6) specifically for the extraction step: reading unstructured text and proposing structured candidates (principals, resources, relationships, conflicts, gaps, questions), each cited to its source and carrying the model's own stated confidence and reasoning. It never activates anything on its own. Every candidate, without exception, requires an explicit human promotion and approval through the same lifecycle every manually authored policy uses. This boundary (AI proposes, a human decides, OPA enforces deterministically) is the single most important sentence in this entire document if it is ever at risk of drifting.

## Why Enterprise Knowledge is deliberately kept separate, and deliberately not yet built

Runtime Authority answers "is this action authorized by our own rules." A large class of real authorization decisions also depends on facts that live in *other* enterprise systems: is this vendor approved, is AML complete, is insurance active, is training current, is budget available. These are not authority-structure facts; they are external, live, systems-of-record facts. Conflating "what does our policy say" with "what does some other system currently claim to be true" would make Runtime Authority's own evaluation non-deterministic in a way that breaks the platform's central guarantee (Section "Why OPA," above): a fact fetched live from an external system, at the moment of decision, is a different kind of dependency than a compiled, versioned, signed policy. This is why Milestone 9's own Workstream 3 designs Enterprise Knowledge's architecture without building it: the how (see `ENTERPRISE_KNOWLEDGE_ARCHITECTURE.md`) has to preserve determinism and Runtime Authority's own evaluation guarantees, not just add a "call an API" step naively.

## Why multi-tenancy was retrofitted rather than designed in from day one, and why that was the right call in sequence

The platform's early milestones (pre-Milestone-2) built a genuinely single-tenant system, deliberately, to prove the Runtime Authority mechanism itself worked before spending engineering effort on isolation nobody needed yet with zero real customers. Milestone 2 (Multi-Tenant Foundation) then retrofitted organization-scoping across Runtime Policies, OPA packages, and safety checks; Milestone 3 (Enterprise Surface Isolation) closed every remaining gap a dedicated adversarial audit found (Authority Builder, Agent Platform, AI Policy Builder, Blob Storage, Azure AI Search, the SDK, the frontend), and proved it live with a real second organization. This sequencing, prove the mechanism, then isolate it, was a deliberate choice, not an oversight later corrected; building isolation for a single-tenant proof-of-concept would have been effort spent on a problem that didn't exist yet.

## Why Azure, and why the migration off Render happened in the order it did (Milestones 4-7)

Render was the right zero-cost choice for a pre-revenue pilot phase; it was never going to be the right choice for a genuine enterprise production platform (no private networking control, no managed identity, no first-party AI infrastructure). The migration to Azure was sequenced deliberately: audit first (Milestone 4, find out what was actually true rather than assume), fix every discovered application-level blocker before touching DNS (Milestone 5-6), and only then execute the actual traffic cutover (Milestone 7), each step gated on the previous one's live validation, not on a calendar date. Render was kept running as a rollback target well past the point the new infrastructure looked ready, specifically because "looks ready" and "proven, with real traffic, over real elapsed time" are different claims, and the platform's own engineering discipline throughout this entire history has been to not conflate the two.
